Lab 01 · Trigonometry · Ages 14+

The Unit Circle & the Sine Wave

A point travels around a circle of radius 1. Its height is sin θ and its horizontal distance is cos θ. Plot the height against the angle and you get a wave.

What's happening

The radius is always 1, so the yellow leg (sin θ) and white leg (cos θ) form a right triangle with hypotenuse 1. That's why sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 for every angle.

Try this

  • Where is sin θ biggest? Where is it zero?
  • Turn on the cosine wave. It's the same shape — shifted by how many degrees?
  • Click 90°. Why does tan θ become “undefined”?
Lab 02 · Geometry · Ages 12+

Pythagoras, Proved by Sliding

For any right triangle, the square on the longest side equals the other two squares added together. Change the triangle on the left, then play the proof on the right.

What's happening

The big square and the four triangles never change size, so the yellow area left over can't change either. At the start it's one tilted square, c². After sliding, it's two squares, a² + b². Same area, so a² + b² = c².

Try this

  • Count the unit squares in a² and b² on the left. Do they add up to c²?
  • Make a = b. What shape do the two yellow squares make?
  • Pause halfway with the slider. Is the yellow area still the same?
Lab 03 · Measurement · Ages 11+

Why a Circle's Area is πr²

Cut a circle into equal slices and lay them top-to-tail. Half the crust goes on top, half on the bottom, and the shape starts to look like a rectangle that is πr wide and r tall.

Width
Height
Width × height
πr²
How close?

What's happening

The slices keep the circle's whole area. As you add slices, the bumpy edges flatten out: the width approaches half the circumference (½ × 2πr = πr) and the height approaches r. So area = πr × r = πr².

Try this

  • Start with 4 slices. Does it look like a rectangle? Now try 60.
  • Watch “How close?” as you add slices. How many do you need for 99%?
  • Double the radius from 2.5 to 5. What happens to the area — ×2 or ×4?
